Each one seemed like something conjured from a dream—white hoods laced in red, green trumpets catching the light, strange and beautiful and a little bit eerie. They’ve always reminded me of something from Alice in Wonderland—creatures that could talk if they wished, fed by insects who linger too long.

The first time I saw pitcher plants was more than twenty years ago at Weeks Bay, walking with my parents and children. Seeing them again felt like coming full circle—time folding in on itself in the humid air.

Less than three percent of their habitat remains now. Still, in this quiet place the pitcher plants endure—alive, extraordinary, and wholly themselves. I was fascinated not just by the plants themselves, but also by the associated wildlife community. I heard frogs calling & birds twittering. What struck me most though were all the insects that seemed to be courting fate in their proximity to these insectivorous plants.

All it takes is one insect to perch inside a pitcher plant’s smooth-walled and slippery funnel, no doubt on its way toward the soup of digestive enzymes below that will convert it into a meal, providing the plant with nitrogen & other nutrients naturally scarce in this acidic environment. Fittingly, other insect predators take advantage of the pitcher plant’s allure. Several species of spider lay hidden from view just below the plant’s treacherously waxy rim, ready to steal vulnerable prey. Others were more bold, weaving webs across the pitcher plants to divert dinner into their own death traps. The longer I watched, the more I understood. And the more I understood, the more fantastical it became!

Each one seemed like something conjured from a dream—white hoods laced in red, green trumpets catching the light, strange and beautiful and a little bit eerie. They’ve always reminded me of something from Alice in Wonderland—creatures that could talk if they wished, fed by insects who linger too long.

The first time I saw pitcher plants was more than twenty years ago at Weeks Bay, walking with my parents and children. Seeing them again felt like coming full circle—time folding in on itself in the humid air.

Less than three percent of their habitat remains now. Still, in this quiet place the pitcher plants endure—alive, extraordinary, and wholly themselves. I was fascinated not just by the plants themselves, but also by the associated wildlife community. I heard frogs calling & birds twittering. What struck me most though were all the insects that seemed to be courting fate in their proximity to these insectivorous plants.

All it takes is one insect to perch inside a pitcher plant’s smooth-walled and slippery funnel, no doubt on its way toward the soup of digestive enzymes below that will convert it into a meal, providing the plant with nitrogen & other nutrients naturally scarce in this acidic environment. Fittingly, other insect predators take advantage of the pitcher plant’s allure. Several species of spider lay hidden from view just below the plant’s treacherously waxy rim, ready to steal vulnerable prey. Others were more bold, weaving webs across the pitcher plants to divert dinner into their own death traps. The longer I watched, the more I understood. And the more I understood, the more fantastical it became!
